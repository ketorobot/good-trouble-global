01

Overall assessment

+

Yes—I think Good Trouble is worth pursuing. But I would change how you ask someone to become the first customer before building much more.

You have a coherent product vision, a distinctive brand, and a tangible demo. What is still unproven is whether a particular owner will pay enough for a particular implementation—and whether you can deliver and support it profitably. A polished demo can help establish that, but it does not establish it by itself.

This assessment covers the project context, live homepage, pricing, About and contact pages, the med-spa demo, and the personal injury site. It is an outside commercial assessment; I have not audited the backend or your current outreach conversion numbers. Website observations reflect the September 12 review.

02

The strongest part of the business

+

The strongest part of the business is the combination of software and your involvement. An owner can already buy scheduling, messaging, campaigns, and AI reception. Zenoti and Boulevard actively advertise overlapping capabilities, so connected software with AI is not enough to distinguish you on its own. That is their advertised offering, not my independent validation of their performance.

Boulevard medical spa software ↗

Your opportunity is making those capabilities work around the specific practice: understanding its bottleneck, implementing the workflow, handling exceptions, and improving it with the owner.

The platform makes that work repeatable. You and Augie make it relevant and trustworthy. I would make that relationship more visible.

The important competitive question is: Why would this owner pay you instead of asking their staff or existing software provider to handle it? A specific problem you can solve better is a stronger answer than a longer feature list.

03

Make the first purchase concrete

+

My biggest broad-stroke change is to keep the platform vision, but make the first purchase smaller and more concrete. I understand that you want to sell the whole system, and I agree with preserving that ambition. However, an unfamiliar company asking to influence someone’s entire practice creates a substantial trust hurdle.

Let owners understand the whole system, then buy an initial implementation with:

  • One agreed business problem.
  • A defined scope and launch plan.
  • A baseline and measurable success criteria.
  • Clear responsibilities, costs, and a continuation decision.

For one spa, that might be after-hours inquiry handling. For another, unbooked consultations or filling cancellations. Reactivation remains a good option when their database and permissions make it appropriate.

That first implementation should demonstrate how the connected platform works. Expansion follows evidence.

I would also put boundaries around customization. “We build whatever you need” is attractive to buyers but can become unlimited development on a fixed subscription. Standardize the underlying workflows, define what adaptation includes, and price substantial new work separately.

04

Reconcile the pricing and commitment

+

The pricing page currently makes the first-client hurdle considerably higher. It publicly lists packages at $2,500, $5,500, and $12,500 per month, a $2,500 installation fee, and month-to-month terms after 90 days. As written, the entry package appears to imply a $10,000 initial three-month commitment including installation. That is a meaningful bet on a company still seeking its first client.

Source: Live pricing page ↗

I am not saying those prices cannot be earned. I am saying the proof and buying experience need to support them.

  • Ignite’s three listed components total $2,200 individually, while the package costs $2,500. Any additional value needs explaining.
  • “Most chosen” needs an actual customer basis.
  • “We bring your numbers—you don’t prepare a thing” promises access and preparation that a cold prospect may not have provided.

The project pack says approved pricing was not recovered, so these may be older choices. Either way, prospects can see them today.

I would test a paid, tightly scoped initial engagement before deciding the whole business needs cheaper pricing. Set its price from the work, delivery costs, and value involved; do not pick a discount simply to get a yes.

05

Keep med spas as the main focus

+

You already have sector-specific materials, outreach experience, and Augie physically meeting businesses in Miami. Those relationships are useful assets.

Personal injury is worth exploring if you have a warm decision-maker with a concrete need and willingness to pay. I would not fund a second full product direction just because the market looks attractive. It introduces a different buyer, workflow, integration environment, and standard of proof.

The personal injury site also stretches the promise too far: learning how a firm “wins cases” suggests more than improving intake and follow-up. I would anchor that offer in the operational result you can actually deliver.

06

Improve the website through commercial clarity

+

The website looks credible. The midnight-and-brass palette and typography feel appropriate for premium practices. The homepage and demo visibly belong together. I would keep that identity.

The homepage communicates the ambition, but “connected intelligence” and “judgment” require interpretation. I would give visitors an immediately understandable example of what changes during an ordinary working day. The existing start-with-the-biggest-gap approach is good; I would make that the center of the buying story.

Source: Homepage ↗

I would also clarify what stays in their existing software, what you connect, what staff must do, and what requires approval. Those are likely buying questions, even when prospects do not articulate them.

A couple of credibility issues need attention:

  • The About page lists three additional team members and describes work for leading aesthetic practices. The supplied context describes a two-person business seeking its first client. There may be a valid explanation, but those claims should match reality.
  • The homepage’s compliance claims need supporting documentation. I have not verified that documentation.

Source: About page ↗

Being two accessible founders can be a selling point. You do not need the appearance of a larger organization to make the offer compelling.

07

Build the demo around one convincing workflow

+

The demo’s best idea is “Needs you” versus “Handled.” That communicates something owners can want immediately: visibility into what happened and a short list of decisions requiring their attention. The after-hours conversation ending in a booking also makes the benefit understandable. Keep those.

Source: Med-spa demo ↗

Where I would improve the demo:

  • Reach the useful moment faster. The opening feature presentation delays the experience.
  • Guide the first visit. Fifteen tools are useful for exploration, but a prospect should encounter one convincing workflow before browsing the catalog.
  • Demonstrate adaptability. Let an owner change a priority and see the proposed actions change. The interactions I inspected communicate automation more clearly than adaptation.
  • Explain consequential approvals. Before accepting a booking action or inventory reorder, show what will happen and why.
  • Distinguish illustrative numbers from results throughout. The front-desk module correctly labels sample data. I would make that equally apparent on the main dashboard.

I would want the first minute to show a problem, the system’s response, the owner’s control, and the outcome.

08

Change the review routing concept

+

One actual product change I would prioritize is review routing. The sites explicitly describe sending satisfied customers toward public reviews while directing unhappy customers privately. Google prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews.

Keep private service recovery, but make review invitations independent of sentiment. This affects both the offer and the demo, rather than merely wording.

09

Focus the next effort on the first client

+

For getting the first client, I would focus your next effort on learning where the sale stalls. Without current numbers, I cannot honestly tell you whether email, Instagram, or walk-ins are working. Track decision-maker conversations, demos held, proposals, and paid starts by channel. Record the actual objections.

Augie’s visits are especially useful if they produce a conversation about the last missed inquiry, an unresolved follow-up problem, or something the owner already pays staff to handle. A compliment about the app is encouraging; willingness to share the workflow and pay to improve it is stronger evidence.

My recommendation is to pursue one practice with a pressing problem, compatible systems, and an owner willing to work closely with you. Deliver a bounded improvement, measure it honestly, and use that experience to sharpen the platform and the sales story.

I would keep building Good Trouble. I would make the next milestone a customer who can explain exactly why paying you was worthwhile.
